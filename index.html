How German sounds

German spelling is almost perfectly regular. Learn these and you can pronounce any word you have never seen before — which is more than English or French will ever give you.

Three sounds Arabic already gave you

The hard ch in Buch and Nacht is the Arabic خ — you have had it since you were a child, and English speakers spend years on it. The h in Haus and Hand is the Arabic ه, which French speakers cannot say at all. And the German r sits in the throat, close to a soft غ. You start ahead on all three.

Four things to actually work on

Arabic has no p, so Papier and Preis will come out as Babier and Breis — press the lips and release. Arabic has no v sound as in German w, so Wasser needs the English v. The vowels ö and ü do not exist in Arabic: round your lips for u, then say ee. And German has short and long vowels that change meaning — Stadt and Staat are different words.

Where the stress goes

Almost always the first syllable: ARbeit, LERnen, WASser. Except for verbs starting with be-, ver-, er-, ent-, ge-, where it moves past the prefix: verSTEhen, beKOMmen. And separable prefixes always take the stress: ANrufen, AUFstehen — which is how you can hear that a verb separates before anyone teaches you.

The one rule that decides everything

German subtitles, or none at all. Never Arabic, never English, never French. In the study that measured this, learners watching with subtitles in their own language improved their listening by exactly zero. With German subtitles, about seventeen percent. If something is only watchable with Arabic subtitles, it is the wrong thing to watch — go down a level and come back to it later.

Take the exam in pieces

The Goethe certificate splits into four modules — reading, listening, writing and speaking — and you can sit them separately. Watching builds listening and reading much faster than writing and speaking, so sit those two first, around month twelve, and collect two real certificates while you build the other two.
